A head of school asked me last year what her board's mental health strategy should look like. It was a reasonable question with an awkward answer: she does not have a board, so she does not have a strategy, and nobody in the system is going to notice that or fund it.

Ontario's publicly funded boards are required to maintain a multi-year mental health strategy. The province funds a dedicated mental health lead in every district, and a provincial implementation team supports those leads with programming, training, and evidence review. That structure is not a nice-to-have layered on top of teaching. It is the thing that decides which programs get used, trains the staff who deliver them, and holds the referral pathways together.

Independent schools have none of it. Not a reduced version. None.

## What the gap actually consists of

It helps to be specific, because "less support" is easy to nod along to and hard to act on. Four things are missing.

**Someone whose job this is.** In a public board, when a Grade 7 cohort starts showing a pattern, there is a person who notices, decides what to do, and has budget. In an independent school that person is usually the head, or a part-time guidance role covering three hundred students, or nobody.

**Programming that has been chosen rather than found.** Public boards work from a vetted set of resources. Independent schools tend to run whatever a parent recommended, a vendor cold-emailed, or a teacher found free online. Some of it is fine. Nobody has checked.

**Staff training as a standing item.** The evidence on school mental health is consistent about one thing: the intervention that matters most is the ordinary adult in the room responding differently. That requires training, repeated, not a single PD morning in 2019.

**Referral pathways settled in advance.** When a student discloses something serious, the question of who they are handed to should already be answered. In schools with a board structure, it usually is. Elsewhere it gets decided in a corridor, by whoever is standing there.

## The part nobody budgets for

The student need is the same. Independent schools serve the same adolescents in the same province through the same rise in anxiety. What differs is the staff exposure.

A smaller school means each teacher carries more non-teaching duties, knows each student better, and absorbs more of what those students are dealing with. There is no federation support structure, and there is often no colleague who does the same job to compare notes with. When someone leaves, they take a term of stability with them, and independent schools rarely have the bench to absorb that.

> The teacher is the mental health system. That is true in every school. In a school without a board structure behind it, it is true and nothing else is.

## What closing part of it looks like

You cannot replicate a provincial implementation team, and you should not try. What you can do is buy the two or three components that carry the most weight.

In rough order of return: train the staff, because it changes what happens in every classroom every day rather than in one assembly. Settle the referral protocol in writing, because it costs nothing and it is the thing you will most regret not having. Give parents something specific to do, because for anxiety in particular, what happens at home determines whether school-based work holds. Then, if there is budget left, run student sessions.

Most schools do that list in reverse, because student sessions are the most visible and the easiest to describe to a parent body. It is the least efficient order.

## On price

The reason independent schools underbuy this is rarely conviction. It is that training vendors quote on request, which means the price depends on what they think the school can pay, and a head of school with no comparator cannot tell whether $4,000 is reasonable.

So, for reference: a half-day of staff training should cost roughly what a day of supply coverage costs, not what a consultancy engagement costs. A parent evening is often funded by the parent association rather than the school budget, which means it does not compete with PD spending at all. And a school under 200 students should be paying meaningfully less than a school of 800, because the work is smaller.

If a vendor will not tell you the price before a discovery call, that is information about the vendor.
